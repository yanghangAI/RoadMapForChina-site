## Overview

The Forbidden City — officially the Palace Museum (故宫博物院) — was the imperial palace of China for 24 emperors across the Ming and Qing dynasties, from 1420 to 1912. It is the largest surviving palace complex on earth: 72 hectares, 980 buildings, nearly 9,000 rooms enclosed within a 10-metre-high wall and a 52-metre-wide moat. For almost five centuries, ordinary Chinese were forbidden to enter on pain of death — hence the name.

The complex was designed on a single north-south axis that runs through the geographic and symbolic centre of Beijing. Everything is arranged in pairs around this axis — symmetry is the architectural language of imperial power. The outer court (southern half) held the great ceremonial halls where the emperor ruled; the inner court (northern half) was the private domain of the imperial family, its concubines, and the eunuchs who administered it.

Construction began in 1406 under the Yongle Emperor, who moved the capital from Nanjing to Beijing and built the palace using one million workers over 14 years. The timber came from the forests of Sichuan and Yunnan; the stone from quarries outside Beijing, hauled to the city on ice roads in winter. Almost every hall has been rebuilt at least once following fires — the last major conflagration destroyed the Hall of Supreme Harmony in 1679 — but the layout has remained unchanged since the 15th century.

In 1925, after the last Qing emperor Puyi was expelled from the inner court, the palace was opened to the public and declared a museum. The collection it holds — paintings, ceramics, bronzes, calligraphy, imperial robes, jade — numbers over 1.86 million objects. It is, by any measure, the greatest collection of Chinese imperial art in existence.

## What to See & Do

- **Hall of Supreme Harmony (太和殿)** — the largest wooden structure in China, where the emperor held court for major ceremonies. The raised marble terrace, the cloud of incense braziers, the golden roof with its line of decorative figures — this is the image of imperial China. Do not miss it.
- **Hall of Clocks and Watches (钟表馆)** — a separate ticket (¥10) but worth it: a collection of 18th-century European and Chinese mechanical clocks, many still working, commissioned by Qianlong Emperor. Strange and beautiful objects.
- **Treasure Gallery (珍宝馆)** — another separate ticket but contains the imperial jewellery, ceremonial objects, and the famous Qianlong Emperor's study, reassembled exactly as it was.
- **The side courtyards** — walk east or west off the main axis into the lesser-visited residential courtyards. The crowds thin immediately. The architecture is quieter, more domestic, more human.
- **Imperial Garden (御花园)** at the north end — ancient cypress trees, rock gardens, pavilions. The exit is through here; arrive early enough to enjoy it without crowds.
- **Jingshan Park (景山公园)** across the street to the north — ¥2 entry, walk up the artificial hill for the best panoramic view of the entire Forbidden City roofscape.

## Best Time to Visit

July is high season — arrive at opening (8:30am) to beat the tour groups that flood in from 10am. The complex is large enough that the crowds disperse, but the main axis gets very congested by midday. Early morning light on the yellow roofs is also the best for photography.

## Practical Tips

- **Tickets:** ¥60 in peak season (Apr–Oct). **Must book online in advance** at [gugong.dpm.org.cn](https://gugong.dpm.org.cn) — daily cap of 40,000 visitors, sells out weeks ahead in July. Bring your passport, which is required for ticket collection.
- **Opening hours:** 8:30am–5:00pm (last entry 4:00pm). Closed Mondays.
- **Bags:** No large bags or suitcases allowed inside. Store at the south gate cloakroom (¥10, reclaim on exit from the north gate — tell them your exit point when storing).
- **Photography:** Allowed in all open courtyards and gardens. Not allowed inside the exhibition halls. The rule is enforced.
- **Duration:** 3 hours covers the main axis and one side courtyard comfortably. The full complex takes a full day.
- **Getting there:** Tiananmen South gate is the main entrance — enter from Tiananmen Square side. Exit from the north (Shenwu Gate) and walk into Jingshan Park.
- **Audio guide:** Available at the south gate (¥40 + ¥100 deposit). Well worth it — the buildings are labelled in Chinese only and the context is everything.

## Why It's Worth It

Standing in the Hall of Supreme Harmony courtyard — a space that holds 100,000 people and was designed to make every individual feel small before imperial power — is one of those experiences that no photograph prepares you for. The sheer scale is a deliberate political statement, executed with architectural perfection. For a first-time visitor to China, this is the single place that makes the weight of Chinese history physically legible.
