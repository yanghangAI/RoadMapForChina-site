## Overview

798 Art District (大山子艺术区) is Beijing's contemporary art hub, built inside a decommissioned state military electronics factory complex in the Dashanzi neighbourhood of Chaoyang. The factories were built in the early 1950s by East German engineers in the Bauhaus industrial style — wide saw-tooth skylights, exposed brick, massive concrete frames — and operated as Factory 718, producing electronics for the Chinese military.

When production wound down in the 1980s and 90s, artists began renting the empty workshops. By the early 2000s the area had developed into a concentration of galleries, studios, design firms, and cafés that attracted international attention. The Beijing authorities, initially planning to demolish the complex for residential development, eventually recognised its value and designated it a cultural district in 2006.

Today 798 contains over 200 galleries and art spaces — from outposts of major international galleries to independent Chinese contemporary art spaces — alongside design boutiques, bookshops, restaurants, and the occasional incongruous souvenir stall. The industrial architecture, still largely intact, gives the whole complex a physical coherence that Beijing's newer cultural districts lack.

## What to See & Do

- **Pace Beijing** and **Ullens Center for Contemporary Art (UCCA)** — two of the most serious exhibition spaces in the district. UCCA in particular has hosted major international and Chinese contemporary art exhibitions since 2007. Check what's showing before you visit.
- **The factory streets themselves** — the open walkways between the old workshop buildings, with their Bauhaus proportions and traces of Cultural Revolution slogans still painted on the walls, are as interesting as anything inside the galleries. Walk slowly.
- **Street art and large-scale installations** — scattered throughout the outdoor spaces. Changes regularly.
- **798 Space (798空间)** — the original gallery that gave the district its name, inside a particularly well-preserved factory hall. Worth walking through for the architecture alone.
- **Bookshops** — several independent bookshops with strong Chinese contemporary art sections. Good for design books, art theory, and unusual gifts.
- **Cafés** — the best ones are inside converted factory spaces. Order a coffee, sit under a 10-metre Bauhaus roof, and recover from jet lag.

## Best Time to Visit

Late afternoon on arrival day — as a jet-lag walk it is ideal: low-pressure, visually engaging, 5 minutes from Jiangtai Lu. Most galleries are open until 6pm. The outdoor spaces are pleasant in early evening. Avoid Monday, when many galleries are closed.

## Practical Tips

- **Entry:** Free to enter the district. Individual galleries may charge ¥20–60 for major exhibitions; UCCA typically ¥100.
- **Opening hours:** Most galleries 10am–6pm, closed Monday. The outdoor streets and cafés are accessible any time.
- **Getting there:** 5min drive from Jiangtai Lu / 丽都饭店. By subway: Jiuxianqiao station (Line 14), then 10min walk.
- **Duration:** 1.5–2 hours for a relaxed walk with one or two gallery visits.
- **What to skip:** The tourist-facing shops selling mass-produced "art" and souvenirs near the main entrance. Walk deeper into the complex.

## Why It's Worth It

798 is a good first-hour introduction to contemporary China — not the ancient monuments or the political spectacles, but the country's artistic conversation with its own recent past and its place in the global present. The Bauhaus-meets-Cultural-Revolution architecture is genuinely unlike anything in Europe, and the quality of the gallery programme is serious. For a jet-lag afternoon, it is close to perfect.
